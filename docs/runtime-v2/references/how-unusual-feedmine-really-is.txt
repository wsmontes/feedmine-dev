# How unusual FeedMine really is

## What the search says

I do **not** think you are imagining the problem. Over the last five years, there has clearly been renewed effort around taking back control of discovery from algorithmic platforms. But almost every serious attempt I found tackles only **one slice** of the problem: a better reader, a better open-social browser, a better feed-generator, a better indie-web directory, or a better news-comparison layer. I did **not** find a clear mainstream product that combines all of those into a very large, multilingual, cross-media, user-facing public catalog that is meant to make the open web feel as easy and habit-forming as infinite-scroll social media. That combination still looks rare. citeturn17view7turn18view4turn18view3turn16view6turn17view0turn16view7turn18view0

That matters, because it changes the question. The question is probably **not** “has nobody ever tried to improve feed-based discovery?” Many people have. The better question is “has anyone put together the same package I’m putting together?” Based on what is publicly visible today, the answer looks closer to **no** than to yes. The space is active, but it is fragmented. citeturn18view4turn18view3turn16view2turn16view1turn16view6turn18view1turn16view7

So my honest read is this: **FeedMine is not protocol-level innovation, but it may well be product-level and institution-level innovation.** RSS, OPML, catalogs, chronological feeds, curation, and public submission flows all already exist. What I do not see much of is the **combination** of large-scale source discovery, catalog governance, open-web distribution, and “scrollable serendipity” in one coherent product. citeturn11search0turn16view6turn17view2turn18view4turn18view3turn16view2

## The products that come closest

The closest recent cluster is what *The Verge* called the new generation of **“timeline apps.”** In 2025 it grouped together Tapestry, Reeder, Feeeed, and Surf as products that aggregate blogs, podcasts, YouTube, and social feeds into one personal stream. That is important evidence that your intuition is pointed at a real direction of travel, not a fantasy. The appetite for “one place to follow the web” is real. citeturn14search14

Among those, **Tapestry** is especially relevant. Its own positioning is almost a manifesto for post-platform reading: it combines social services, RSS feeds, podcasts, YouTube channels, and more into a **unified, chronological timeline**, with no recommendation algorithm deciding what you should see. It also exposes a connector model, which means the system is designed to keep expanding as long as a source has some public feed-like surface. citeturn18view4turn16view3

**Reeder** moved in a similar direction. The new version is no longer just an RSS app; it explicitly says it supports videos, podcasts, and social media posts in one unified timeline, and it lets any tag become a public feed that updates automatically. That is very close to your intuition that the future is not “just a feed reader,” but a structured way to follow the web itself. citeturn18view3

**Surf**, from Flipboard, is the strongest sign that even larger and more experienced teams now think the center of gravity is shifting toward open, composable feeds. Surf describes itself as a browser for the open social web; by April 2026 it had launched “social websites” powered by feeds whose sources, filters, and moderation are controlled by the creator, not by a centralized black-box algorithm. Flipboard’s press materials explicitly frame this as decentralizing the value of social media and giving more power back to creators and communities. *The Verge* described Surf as a client for federated networks, a feed reader for websites, podcasts and YouTube channels, and a tool for creating and following curated feeds, with search spanning billions of posts across ActivityPub, AT Protocol, and the web. citeturn16view2turn17view7

**Folo** is another close cousin, but from a different angle. Its pitch is “the AI RSS Reader,” and its repo describes an “open information playground” that organizes content into one timeline, with shared lists, collections, discovery, dynamic content support, and optional AI features layered on top. That tells you something important: the ecosystem is increasingly aware that plain subscription management is not enough. Readers now feel pressure to solve **discovery, filtering, and synthesis**, not merely ingestion. citeturn18view2turn16view1

What none of these fully seems to be, though, is the thing you are describing: a **very large public source catalog** that itself becomes part of the product and the movement. Tapestry and Reeder are mostly reader experiences. Surf comes closest on community curation, but its center of gravity is the open social web rather than a giant independent source catalog. Folo is trying to modernize the feed reader with AI, but it looks more like an app-plus-discovery layer than a public multilingual catalog institution. citeturn18view4turn18view3turn17view7turn16view1turn18view2

## The important adjacent models

There are also projects that solve **part** of your problem in a deeper way than the timeline apps do.

One obvious example is **RSSHub**. RSSHub calls itself the world’s largest RSS network, with over 5,000 global instances delivering content from many kinds of sources. Its browser extension, RSSHub Radar, is explicitly built to discover and subscribe to RSS or RSSHub-compatible feeds on the current page. This is not a public editorial catalog in the way you seem to want FeedMine to become, but it is absolutely proof that brute-force feed extraction and feed normalization at scale is a real and living ecosystem, not an eccentric dead-end. citeturn4search0turn20view2

Another adjacent model is **Podcast Index**, which is probably the strongest evidence that an open, public index can work when it is constrained to one medium. The project exists, in its own words, to “preserve, protect and extend the open, independent podcasting ecosystem,” and outside descriptions now refer to it as an open database with more than 4 million podcasts and a public API that other apps can use. That matters because it shows that an open catalog can become infrastructure. It also shows why your instinct toward a public source directory is not crazy; it already works in podcasting. citeturn17view0turn17view1turn20view0

On the human-curated web-discovery side, **Blogroll.org**, **Marginalia**, and **Kagi Small Web** all point toward the same cultural hole you are trying to fill. Blogroll.org describes itself as a living map of independent blogs, openly curated to make the independent web easier to explore. Marginalia prioritizes non-commercial content and positions itself as a search-and-discovery engine for “lost old websites.” Kagi Small Web is even more revealing: it is an open-source curated initiative integrated into Kagi search, with contribution workflows in GitHub, explicit curation rules, an RSS feed, and even a separate “Small YouTube” concept. In other words, the demand to surface overlooked human-made sources is real enough that multiple projects are now building special machinery just for that. citeturn18view1turn16view5turn18view0turn17view2turn13search0

Then there are the **news-focused** products: **Ground News**, **SmartNews**, **Particle**, and the now-shuttered **Artifact**. Ground News says it aggregates over 50,000 sources and focuses on bias, blind spots, and source comparison. SmartNews says it works from thousands of media partners and blends editorial and algorithmic curation. Particle organizes large clusters of news coverage into AI-composed “Stories” with summaries, perspective tools, and publisher partnerships. Artifact tried a strongly personalized, AI-driven news discovery experience, built a loyal core audience, and still shut down after concluding that the market opportunity was not large enough in that form. These are crucial comparators because they prove there is money, talent, and repeated experimentation in the broader “organize the web’s information better” space — but they also show that most of the energy is captured inside **news-only**, **AI-summary**, or **publisher-partnership** products rather than open-web catalog products. citeturn20view1turn19view2turn19view3turn19view0turn19view1turn17view5turn17view6

## Why nobody has fully cracked this yet

The simplest answer is that the **reader problem** is easier than the **catalog problem**. Building a polished app that merges feeds is difficult but tractable. Building and maintaining a large, multilingual, cross-media, high-quality source inventory is messier, more editorial, and more political. Kagi Small Web’s own explanation is telling here: it has explicit inclusion rules, says non-English is harder to curate, and openly admits that curating the small web means “putting our reputation on the line.” That is not a software problem anymore. It is a governance problem. citeturn18view0

The second reason is that the web is still rich in feeds, but the feeds are **unevenly exposed and unevenly discoverable**. A 2026 survey of the top 500,000 sites found more than 534,000 parseable feeds and found that 35.9% of sites exposed feed autodiscovery, which is a big number — but also means most sites still do **not** advertise feeds in the easiest possible way. On top of that, real-world implementations are messy enough that even Discourse users are still running into autodiscovery problems in 2026. So the brute-force work you describe is not a sign that you are doing something foolish; it is a sign that the open web remains abundant but structurally inconsistent. citeturn17view3turn11search1turn11search0

The third reason is that social and search platforms have become worse partners for publishers and independent creators, which makes open-web discovery more necessary **and** harder at the same time. The Reuters Institute’s 2025 Digital News Report found that social and video platforms are further diminishing the influence of institutional journalism, while Oxford’s 2026 report on publisher traffic said Google Search referrals had fallen 33% year-over-year and Google Discover referrals 21% across more than 2,500 news sites, with managers expecting search referrals to fall another 43% over the next three years. In plain English: independent discovery is more valuable now, but the surrounding ecosystem is less likely to hand it to you for free. citeturn10search0turn17view4turn10search3

The fourth reason is that as soon as discovery becomes social or public, **moderation and dispute resolution** show up. Surf explicitly talks about sources, filters, and moderation being controlled by feed creators. Artifact, when shutting down, said it had to remove certain social features because they required moderation and oversight the team would no longer have staff to support. That is the hidden cost of becoming an institution rather than just an app. The moment FeedMine lets people correct sources, contest categorization, or request removal, it enters governance territory. citeturn16view2turn17view5

The fifth reason is business-model gravity. Kagi monetizes via paid search and API access. SmartNews monetizes via advertising and partnerships. Ground News monetizes analysis and subscriptions. Folo layers in AI. Particle is trying partnerships plus AI organization. Artifact could not find sufficient market opportunity in time. What this suggests is not that your idea is doomed; it suggests that **the market rewards narrower wedges first**. Many teams pick one tractable angle — AI news summaries, feed reading, search, podcast indexing, indie-web discovery — because the full-stack vision is harder to explain, govern, and finance. citeturn18view0turn19view3turn20view1turn16view1turn19view0turn17view5turn17view6

## What looks genuinely new in FeedMine

Based on your description, the most original thing about FeedMine is **not** that it uses feeds. The most original thing is that it seems to treat feeds as the raw material for rebuilding **discovery itself**.

That sounds abstract, so I’ll make it concrete. The comparable products usually optimize one of these experiences: “read what you already follow,” “find a better source,” “compare coverage,” “follow open-social communities,” or “discover indie sites.” FeedMine, as you’ve described it, is trying to combine those into a single habit loop: gather a giant open-web catalog, normalize it across languages and media, let the user enter through a familiar scrolling interface, and then use catalog curation to create serendipitous encounters with sources the user would never have searched for directly. I did not find a recent product that clearly does **that exact stack**. citeturn18view4turn18view3turn17view7turn16view6turn18view1turn16view7

A second potentially original piece is your instinct that this has to become a **movement or institution**, not just an app. That sounds grandiose until you compare it with the projects that actually endure. Kagi Small Web uses public contribution rules. Blogroll relies on a curator with explicit judgment. Podcast Index acts like public infrastructure for an ecosystem. Wikipedia’s long-term strength comes from policy, process, and conflict handling, not just software. The recurring lesson in the landscape is that as soon as discovery becomes public and meaningful, governance matters as much as UX. On that front, your instinct is not eccentric at all — it is probably the mature reading of the problem. citeturn18view0turn18view1turn17view0turn15search1turn15search18turn15search22

A third unusual piece is the **catalog-first** mindset. Plenary has an open repository of curated RSS and OPML feeds, but by its own description that collection is on the order of hundreds of recommended feeds plus a few hundred country news sources, and it exists mainly to help first-time users with discoverability. That is a meaningful precedent, but it is still much smaller and narrower than what you are describing. In the current landscape, large public catalogs mostly exist either for a single medium, like podcasts, or inside closed commercial apps, like news aggregators. A public, multi-medium, editorially governed source catalog is still a mostly open space. citeturn16view6turn20view0turn19view3turn20view1

So if you want the sharp version of the answer: **FeedMine looks less like a new protocol and more like a new synthesis.** In software, that still counts. A lot of important products are not based on unprecedented primitives; they are based on assembling old primitives under a new theory of use. The search results I found support exactly that reading. citeturn18view4turn18view3turn16view2turn16view1turn18view0turn17view0

## The verdict I would give you

I would **not** tell you this is obviously doomed because “if it were possible, somebody would have done it already.” The internet is full of adjacent attempts, and that is actually encouraging. It means your direction is legible. The more interesting fact is that those attempts keep stopping short of the full combination you care about. Some stop at reading, some stop at search, some stop at podcasts, some stop at news, and some stop at “small web” hand-curation. That pattern usually means there is still an unclaimed product shape here. citeturn18view4turn17view7turn16view1turn17view0turn18view1turn16view7

At the same time, the research does **not** support the comforting fantasy that the hard part is technical. The hard part appears to be editorial durability: inclusion rules, removals, multilingual quality control, handling spam, handling paywalls, building trust with source owners, and turning a giant catalog into something that feels alive rather than archival. That is exactly why it feels like a movement problem to you. The closest projects all point in that same direction. citeturn18view0turn17view5turn20view0turn17view2turn16view2

So my sincere conclusion is this: **yes, there are real precedents — but no, what you are describing does not look ordinary.** It looks unusual not because the individual tools are exotic, but because you are trying to turn old open-web mechanics into a modern discovery institution. That is a rarer ambition than “build a better feed reader,” and the current landscape still leaves room for it. citeturn18view4turn18view3turn17view7turn16view6turn18view0turn17view0
